# Figure list and captions

Generated by `make_figures.py` from the files in `measured/` and the NEC model.
All five are 300 dpi PNG, drawn so that line style distinguishes every series
independently of colour, so they survive greyscale reproduction.

**Figure 1** — `fig1_model_vs_measured.png`

> Resistance and reactance of the 75 mm monopole: the NEC model against both
> instruments. The reactance curves are indistinguishable from 300 MHz to about
> 1 GHz, which validates the model and the hardware together. Above that the two
> instruments diverge from each other and from the model.

**Figure 2** — `fig2_instrument_divergence.png`

> The same antenna measured on an HP 8753C and a NanoVNA. Above, VSWR from each.
> Below, the difference in reflection coefficient between them, which grows
> steadily with frequency rather than failing at any particular point. The
> shaded band is 850 to 1000 MHz, where the two instruments produce designs
> that differ by half a percent.

**Figure 3** — `fig3_vswr_sensitivity.png`

> Why the same measurement error matters differently depending on where it
> lands. VSWR is a nonlinear function of the reflection coefficient, so a fixed
> error of 0.08 is worth a quarter of a point of VSWR near a match and sixteen
> points near the rim of the chart. The three marked lines are the unmatched
> VSWR of the three bands the article matches; the widest band sits at 668 and
> is far off the right of the plot. This, not frequency, is what decides
> whether two instruments agree on a design.

**Figure 4** — `fig4_matched_result.png`

> The measured monopole matched across 850 to 1000 MHz, with networks designed
> from the 8753C data and every impedance constrained to 20 to 150 ohms so that
> the result is fabricable. Note that both networks are worse than the bare
> antenna at midband and much better at the edges: that is what minimising the
> worst case looks like, and it is why hand design, which gravitates to the
> centre, tends to find the wrong answer.

**Figure 5** — `fig5_new_planes.png`

> Averaged over twelve simulated antennas spanning Q from 2.9 to 9.3 and
> bandwidths from 10 to 50 percent: what a third component buys, according to
> where it sits. Added at the same plane as the existing stub it buys nothing at
> all, to three decimal places. Added at a new plane it buys about half the
> remaining mismatch.

## Photographs worth including

**Figure 6** — the antenna itself. 75 mm of 1 mm wire on a two foot copper
ground plane, fed through an SMA connector. Worth showing precisely because it
is unremarkable: the whole study rests on twenty minutes of work and about a
pound of copper.

**Figure 7** — the two analyser screens, side by side. The 8753C trace and the
NanoVNA trace of the same antenna. Both look like clean, believable
measurements, which is the point being made in the text: nothing in either
display warns you that they disagree.
